# What Have We Accomplished?

## I. The Output: The Fetish of Production

The **fundamental mystification** at the heart of both capitalist and Soviet production is the **the output**.

This seemingly innocent abstraction — "a discrete thing produced by discrete labor"—is not a neutral description of reality. It is an *ideological construct* that naturalizes:

- The separation of producers from one another
- The transformation of social interdependence into private property
- The post-hoc recognition of social labor through market or bureaucratic mediation

**The output is a fetish.** Not in the trivial sense of "wrongly valued," but in the precise sense that it **inverts the actual relations of production**.

Production is *already* social, *already* interdependent, *already* communal in its material character. But the output-form **makes this appear as though** production were individual, and only *becomes* social through subsequent exchange or allocation.

Marx saw this with perfect clarity: "On the basis of exchange values, labour is posited as general only through exchange." The labor of the individual becomes social labor only *after the fact*—post festum—through the mediation of money, the market, the commodity form. The social character of production is *revealed* in exchange, not *presupposed* in it.

The capitalist says: "I made this thing, now let the market decide if it was socially necessary."

The planner says: "I made this thing, now let the bureau decide where it goes."

**Both preserve the illusion** that production creates discrete outputs which *then* enter into social relations. Both treat the social character of production as something to be *imposed* on inherently private labor, rather than something *already present* in the material interdependence of all production.

We have shown that this is not merely a theoretical error. It is a **structural necessity** of property relations—both private and state. The output must exist as a discrete, ownable thing precisely because ownership is the mechanism of coordination. Remove outputs, and you remove the substrate of property itself.

## II. The Theoretical Intervention

We have proposed—cautiously, experimentally—a different abstraction: **the commons**.

Not "commons" in the vague sense of "shared resources" (though it is that too), but as a **technical specification** for post-output coordination.

Marx imagined this in Capital: "Let us now picture to ourselves, by way of change, a community of free individuals, carrying on their work with the means of production in common, in which the labour power of all the different individuals is consciously applied as the combined labour power of the community... The total product of our community is a social product. One portion serves as fresh means of production and remains social. But another portion is consumed by the members as means of subsistence."

**What does this accomplish theoretically?**

### **1. It makes the social character of production explicit from the outset**

The commons does not say "I will produce X and then we'll see if anyone wants it." It says "We need X for this communal purpose."

This is precisely Marx's alternative: "But on this foundation it would be posited as such before exchange; i.e. the exchange of products would in no way be the medium by which the participation of the individual in general production is mediated."

The slot is **a priori social**. It exists within a declared communal structure. Labor that fills it is *already recognized* as social labor, not labor that *becomes* social through subsequent exchange.

This inverts the capitalist sequence:
- NOT: Private labor → Output → Exchange → Social recognition
- BUT: Social need → Slot → Contribution → Completion

**Labor is posited as social before it begins, not after it completes.**

And crucially: "The exchange which originally takes place in production – which would not be an exchange of exchange values but of activities, determined by communal needs and communal purposes – would from the outset include the participation of the individual in the communal world of products."

The slot *is* this exchange of activities. Not exchange of things that have been produced, but coordination of activities *as they are being determined*. The slot declares a communal need; the contribution is an activity determined by that need; the result is participation in communal production—not ownership of a private output.

### **2. It dissolves the producer/consumer antithesis**

If there are no outputs, there are no producers (in the sense of "those who own what they've made"). There are only contributors to communal processes.

Marx articulates this precisely: "What he has bought with his labour is not a specific and particular product, but rather a specific share of the communal production. He therefore has no particular product to exchange. The product does not first have to be transposed into a particular form in order to attain a general character for the individual."

Sometimes you fill slots in my commons. Sometimes I fill slots in yours. Neither of us "owns" the result. We are **co-composers** of communal infrastructure.

The distinction between "making something for others" and "using what others made" collapses. All production is *participation* in ongoing composition.

"Instead of a division of labour, such as is necessarily created with the exchange of exchange values, there would take place an organization of labour whose consequence would be the participation of the individual in communal consumption."

This is the crucial distinction: **division** of labor vs. **organization** of labor. Division emerges from exchange—the market fragments production into competing units. Organization emerges from presupposed communality—templates compose activities into coherent wholes. The commons is infrastructure for organization, not division.

### **3. It reveals governance as an unsolved political question**

We have not solved governance. We have **refused to hide it**.

Capitalist markets hide governance behind "voluntary exchange"—as if the acceptance of a transaction were not embedded in massive structures of power, property, and violence.

Soviet plans hide governance behind "scientific necessity"—as if bureaucratic decisions were mere technical implementations of objective needs.

**Commons make governance explicit.** Some slots have automatic acceptance rules (transparent logic). Others have governed acceptance (designated authority). Neither pretends to be "natural" or "objective."

The system asks: **Who decides what fits?** And it does not provide a universal answer. It provides a *space* for that question to be answered contextually, explicitly, accountably.

This is not a flaw. This is **honesty about the political nature of production.**

## III. The Practical Program

We have not merely theorized. We have **specified**.

The code is not a metaphor. It is a **working implementation** of:
- Instance tracking (nanoid-based state)
- Status derivation (actual/potential propagation)
- Governance hooks (automatic vs. governed acceptance)

**This is infrastructure.** Not in the sense of "tools for doing the same thing more efficiently," but in the sense of **material substrate for doing something fundamentally different**.

You cannot build communal production on top of GitHub Issues. You cannot coordinate commons using Jira. Not because these tools are bad, but because they **presuppose outputs**:
- Issues → closed with deliverables
- Tasks → completed with results
- Sprints → ship products

They assume production creates things to be owned, then coordinated.

**Commons infrastructure assumes production fills needs within communal templates.**

## IV. The Historical Wager

We are making a bet about the trajectory of coordination technology.

**The capitalist wager:** If we make exchange frictionless enough (blockchain! smart contracts! DeFi!), markets will optimally coordinate everything.

**The socialist wager:** If we compute precisely enough (input-output tables! optimization algorithms! planning software!), we can rationally allocate everything.

**Both wagers accept outputs as given.** They ask: "How do we coordinate the outputs?"

**Our wager:** If we remove outputs entirely and make coordination patterns themselves a commons, a different mode of production becomes possible—one that doesn't reveal social character post-hoc through exchange or plan, but **presupposes it in the structure of production itself**.

This is not a small claim. We are proposing that the **two-hundred-year debate between markets and plans has been asking the wrong question**.

Not: "How should outputs be coordinated?"

But: "Why are we producing outputs at all?"

Marx glimpsed this in 1858: "To the degree that large industry develops, the creation of real wealth comes to depend less on labour time and on the amount of labour employed than on the power of the agencies set in motion during labour time, whose 'powerful effectiveness' is itself in turn out of all proportion to the direct labour time spent on their production, but depends rather on the general state of science and on the progress of technology."

Real wealth is not outputs. It is **the power of coordination itself**—the general intellect, the social knowledge objectified in our productive apparatus. "The development of fixed capital indicates to what degree general social knowledge has become a direct force of production."

The output-form was always an inadequate measure of this social power. But now, when production increasingly consists of manipulating patterns, coordinating knowledge, composing templates—the output-form becomes actively *absurd*. What is the "output" of a well-designed interface? A well-organized codebase? A well-structured coordination pattern?

"The theft of alien labour time, on which the present wealth is based, appears a miserable foundation in face of this new one, created by large-scale industry itself."

We are building infrastructure for this new foundation.

## V. Time Remains Essential

A common misreading: if we abolish the output, have we abolished the need to account for labor time?

No. Marx is explicit: "On the basis of communal production, the determination of time remains, of course, essential. The less time the society requires to produce wheat, cattle etc., the more time it wins for other production, material or mental."

**Economy of time is the basis of all economy.** This does not change. What changes is the *form* of accounting.

"Economy of time, along with the planned distribution of labour time among the various branches of production, remains the first economic law on the basis of communal production. It becomes law, there, to an even higher degree. However, this is essentially different from a measurement of exchange values (labour or products) by labour time."

In capitalism, labor time serves as the measure of *exchange value*—the basis for equivalence in trade. In communal production, labor time serves as the measure of *social coordination*—how much time must society devote to various needs, and how is this time distributed?

The slot does not escape time. Templates specify temporal constraints. Commons require actual labor to complete. But this labor is not measured to determine exchange ratios. It is measured to coordinate social activity—to ensure that communal needs are met with appropriate allocation of communal effort.

**Time as coordination, not as price.**

## VI. What Remains to Be Done

We have **not** solved:

**1. The governance question in general**

We've provided structure for governed acceptance, but not criteria for *who* should govern *what* under *which* conditions. This is correct—there is no universal answer. But it means every commons must solve this anew (or reference governance commons that specify processes).

**2. The value distribution question**

If there are no outputs to price, how is value recognized? How are contributions rewarded? We've dissolved the mechanism (exchange) without replacing it. This is deliberate—but it means commons must experiment with post-exchange distribution logics.

**3. The incentive compatibility question**

Why would someone contribute to a commons rather than produce for exchange? We've changed the infrastructure, but not (yet) the surrounding political economy. Commons must exist within capitalism until they don't. How do they survive the transition?

**4. The conflict resolution question**

What happens when multiple contributions compete for the same slot? When actualness is contested? When templates fork contentiously? We've provided structure, not resolution.

**5. The scale question**

Can this work beyond small communities? Can it coordinate production at the complexity of modern supply chains? We don't know. We've specified the infrastructure, not proven it scales.

## VII. The Claim

Despite these open questions, we claim to have accomplished something real:

**We have built infrastructure that makes post-output, commons-based production technically possible.**

Not inevitable. Not guaranteed. Not without massive political struggle.

But **possible**.

The code exists. The templates can be hashed. The commons can compose. The social character of production can be presupposed.

**What remains is not technical specification but social construction**—the hard work of actually building communal production within and against the forms that currently dominate.

But now, at least, there is infrastructure for it. 

**Slots instead of outputs. Templates instead of trade secrets. Composition instead of exchange. Commons all the way down.**

---

*The philosophers have only interpreted production in various ways. The point, however, is to change it.*

And we have changed—if only in specification, if only in code, if only in possibility—the very **form** of production itself.

That is what we have accomplished.
